You tap your card. Twenty dollars, gone. The coffee shop does not get twenty dollars. Before your cup is even poured, three companies you never chose have taken a cut.

The cafe receives nineteen dollars and thirty-three cents. Sixty-seven cents vanished somewhere between your thumb and the till, and no human being anywhere ever touched a cent of it. So let's follow it. All twenty dollars, every hand it passes through, until there is nothing left.

That sixty-seven cents is not one fee. It is three, stacked on top of each other, collected in the two seconds your card was touching the reader. The reader charges roughly two point six percent, plus fifteen cents, on an in-person tap.

The largest share, about seventy percent, goes to the bank that issued your card. Roughly forty-seven cents. That fee has a name. Interchange. Remember it, because it explains everything later. Your bank did not lend you anything for two seconds of tapping. It is being paid for permission. Permission to move money it was already holding on your behalf.

Seven cents goes to the card network itself. It owns the wires, and it charges rent on the wires. The last thirteen cents stays with the processor. The company whose little white terminal sat on the counter.

Now here is the part almost nobody connects. Your card pays two percent cashback. On this purchase, forty cents. That forty cents does not come from your bank's kindness. It comes out of the forty-seven it just collected. The bank takes forty-seven cents from the cafe, hands you forty, and keeps seven. Your reward is a rebate on a fee somebody else was charged.

Hold that thought. First, the other nineteen dollars, because the cafe does not keep that either.

Roughly a quarter of your twenty is the stuff itself. Five dollars of beans, milk, a paper cup, a lid. But how much of that is actually coffee? Across the whole coffee trade, the people who grow the beans receive around ten percent of what you pay at retail. Ten percent, for the only step nobody else can do.

That is not one villain taking the rest. It is a queue. The mill, the exporter, the shipping line, the importer, the roaster. Six businesses, six margins, all stacked before the bag reaches the back door.

The single biggest slice is not beans, and it is not fees. It is wages. Around a third of the whole thing. Six dollars and forty cents. Then rent. Two dollars and forty cents of your order goes to somebody who has never made you a coffee.

Put that next to the fees everyone complains about. Sixty-seven cents to three financial companies. Two dollars forty to a landlord. Almost four times as much, for a room.

Then everything nobody thinks about. Electricity, insurance, the software, the repair guy. Call it three dollars. Which leaves about two dollars and fifty-three cents, before tax. A healthy independent cafe runs eight to twelve percent net margin. So of your twenty dollars, the business itself keeps roughly two. Ten cents on the dollar.

So the fees were the smallest part. Which makes the next bit stranger.

Here is the turn. The cafe charges twenty dollars whether you tap a rewards card or hand over a twenty-dollar note. Same menu, same price. But it still has to cover the card fee. So it builds the fee into the price on the board. For everybody. Including cash.

Which means the person paying cash just paid the card fee. They funded forty-seven cents of interchange, and received precisely nothing back, because they were not holding a card. Money left one of them, went through the payment system, and arrived in the pocket of the other one.

Economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston measured this in twenty ten. The average cash-using household pays about a hundred and forty-nine dollars a year to card-using households. The average card-using household receives about eleven hundred and thirty-three.

Now sort those households by income, because card spending rises with income, and so do rewards. The lowest-income households pay about twenty-one dollars a year into this. The highest-income households receive about seven hundred and fifty.

Nobody designed this to move money upward. There is no room where that was decided. It is simply what happens when a fee is invisible and a reward is advertised on television.

So. If the fee is inside the price whether you tap or not, paying cash does not opt you out. It only opts you out of the rebate. Take the rebate. But never carry a balance, because two percent back is meaningless against twenty percent interest.

And keep the general rule. When something in a financial system is free to you, it is not free. Somebody standing nearby is paying for it.

You did not spend twenty dollars on coffee. You spent about a dollar twenty on coffee, and the rest on the enormous apparatus required to hand it to you warm. And the forty cents that came back was never a gift. It was your own money, taking a lap through three companies, arriving home slightly smaller than when it left.
